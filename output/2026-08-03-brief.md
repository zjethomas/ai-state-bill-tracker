## Frontier / Foundation Models (20)

### California

**AB 412** -- Generative artificial intelligence: training data: copyrighted materials.  
This bill would require companies that build generative AI models to set up a website portal where copyright holders can ask whether their specific works were used as training data, and to answer within 30 days with a list of any of that owner's works it used. Developers who miss the deadline rack up a separate violation for each additional day and can be sued by the rights owner after a chance to fix the problem, with exemptions for models trained only on data the developer makes freely public.  
*(Summary based on the official digest/abstract.)*

**SB 1106** -- Agentic artificial intelligence.  
This bill adds "agentic" AI — systems that can take actions on their own rather than just generate content — to two existing state reporting requirements: the Office of Emergency Services must address it in its critical-infrastructure risk analysis for the Governor, and the Department of Technology must include it in its annual inventory of high-risk automated systems used or bought by state agencies. Practically, it expands what agencies have to track and report on, without imposing new restrictions on how AI can be used.  
*(Summary based on the official digest/abstract.)*  
Latest action: Read second time and amended. Re-referred to Com. on APPR. · [source](http://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB1106)

**AB 2653** -- State contracts: report: modern foundation models and associated artificial intelligence systems.  
This bill creates a nine-member state working group, led by the Department of Industrial Relations with the Department of Technology, to examine the labor conditions behind how AI foundation models are built — such as the data-labeling and content-moderation work that trains them. The group must deliver a report to the Legislature by December 31, 2027 recommending whether California should attach labor standards to state purchases of AI systems, similar to existing anti-sweatshop rules for apparel contracts; it does not itself change any procurement requirements.  
*(Summary based on the official digest/abstract.)*  
Latest action: In committee: Held under submission. · [source](http://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB2653)

**SB 813** -- California Artificial Intelligence Standards and Safety Commission: artificial intelligence safety standards.  
This bill creates a new California Artificial Intelligence Standards and Safety Commission (by July 1, 2027, under the Business and Consumer Services Agency) tasked with writing the rules for how third-party AI auditors evaluate AI systems, including the standards they must follow, when their registration can be suspended or revoked, and what qualifies them as truly independent. The new commission wouldn't be created if a separate bill (AB 1709) sets up an e-Safety Advisory Commission instead, in which case that body takes on the same duties — and none of it takes effect unless AB 1405 also becomes law.

## Automated Decision-Making & Discrimination (61)

**AB 1979** -- Health care services: artificial intelligence.  
Health care chatbots would be brought under California's medical privacy law, meaning companies offering them for diagnosis, treatment, or managing a patient's health information would face the same restrictions on sharing, selling, or marketing with patient data as doctors and hospitals. Separately, health facilities, clinics, and physician offices would have to take reasonable steps to ensure that when AI clinical decision support tools inform patient care, a licensed clinician still has the final say — with licensing boards able to seek court orders against violators, though the requirement wouldn't cover AI used purely for paperwork or routine patient notifications.  
*(Summary based on the official digest/abstract.)*

**AB 2575** -- Health care services: artificial intelligence.  
Starting July 1, 2027, hospitals, clinics, and doctors' offices using AI-driven clinical decision support tools would have to give clinicians and other staff who use those tools — on request — a list of all such systems in use plus details on how they generate their recommendations, and notify those workers of this right at hire and annually. The bill also bars employers from retaliating against direct-care workers who exercise their own professional judgment (with a complaint route through the Labor Commissioner), and blocks AI developers and deployers sued over patient harm from shifting blame by arguing a clinician should have overridden the system's output.
